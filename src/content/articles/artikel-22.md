Somehow, people keep saying they like going to the cinema but rarely ever go. Cinemas are such precious places. And if you've never been to a cinema all alone, you should definitely try it. It's such an incomparable feeling, leaving the cinema after an intensive film screening that hopefully took you to a completely different world, stepping outside the door, it's already dark and you enter your own reality again without speaking to anybody in between. You know it when you've felt it.

There are beautiful cinemas in every city, and the best way to keep these places alive is simply to keep showing up. All alone, with a good friend or your family member of choice.

In the following, you can read about four films from this year's film festivals that have really stayed with me. Most of them are still waiting for their cinema release, and all of them deserve to be seen on the big theater screen. Perhaps what connects them, film by film, is the very empathetic way they portray their characters dealing with the unexpected: with loss, growing up, or the sudden arrival of something that leaves a lasting mark on what follows.

**MOUSE – by Kelly O'Sullivan and Alex Thompson**

This was my favourite film I saw at this year's Berlinale, a feeling I seemed to hear echoed around me. A loving US indie film about friendship, which can be beautiful and full of trust, but at the same time mean that one person exists in the shadow of the other. It's about introversion, growing up and moving forward. About loss and how it forms you but also creates something new. It's also about becoming estranged from your own mother and finding a fascination in another mother instead. Minnie is drawn to spending time with her best friend's mother Helen, brilliantly played by Sophie Okonedo, and the feeling seems to be mutual. Slowly, the two form a friendship of their own. There is something very warm and gentle about the way this relationship grows. Tip: The US trailer is not good. Don't watch it. It doesn't capture what makes the film so compelling.

**EVERYTIME – by Sandra Wollner**

EVERYTIME is the kind of film that lets you feel things before you understand them. A family drama between real, digital and surreal worlds. The loss of a daughter, older sister and girlfriend sits at its centre, creating a certain heaviness and unease. And yet there is something quietly beautiful in the way the three people left behind become a group simply by being together, each grieving in their own way. They rarely explain what is happening between them. Much of it is there in the glances, the silences and the small shifts between them. A very noticeable and surprising form of cinema, which feels like something you haven't seen yet, especially in the third act. Memory, presence and imagination keep bleeding into each other, creating an emotional world that is not quite stable. A Berlin film where the Tenerife Minecraft-like sun won't go down.

**LA GRADIVA – by Marine Atlan**

If you haven't heard of LA GRADIVA yet, you should immediately watch the French or US trailer. This first feature film by French cinematographer Marine Atlan takes you on a high school trip to Naples and Pompeii, following Toni, Suzanne and James and the dynamics between the three, their classmates and their teacher. Against the ancient city preserved by Vesuvius and the shimmering streets of Naples, feelings, uncertainties and tensions slowly build up. Pompeii gives the film a strange sense of scale: these teenagers move through a place where people lived, loved, fought and died almost two thousand years ago, while dealing with the very immediate dramas of being young. There is something observant about the way the film watches them: who looks at whom, who joins in, who stays on the outside. The acting feels very real, surely helped by the improvised elements the actors brought to their performances. The film is full of visual choices that stay with you. A portrait of youth that I keep thinking about.

**Club Kid – by Jordan Firstman**

When I recently read a comment on Instagram calling CLUB KID "a cemented queer classic", I could feel where it came from. If you're young, even just a little into music or pop culture, there are so many reasons to like this film. For me, one thing was the cameo appearance of Shygirl. The film is a very funny and surprisingly moving portrait of a gay party promoter lost in the intoxicated excess of New York's club scene, until fatherhood enters the picture. Jordan Firstman is the film's writer, director and lead actor, and there is something slightly self-promotional about the whole thing. But then again, it is his charisma that carries a lot of it. The film is very much aware of its own coolness, which should probably be annoying, but somehow isn't. And then there is fatherhood, entering a life that seems almost perfectly designed to avoid being responsible. Maybe that is where the film gets most interesting. Go watch this with a bunch of friends and, for once, get the large popcorn.
